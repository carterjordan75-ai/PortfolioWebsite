Arriving

How the mark gets there, and only that. Plays once — the loop below takes over when it lands.

WHERE it gets to is Layout, further down — the same panel a loader uses. This section used to carry a partial copy of it (gap, spread, scatter, shuffle, turn), which quietly cost the controls it did not copy: cell turn, turning the X and the O separately, and the score line.

Resting

Redraw sends the outline drawing itself on again, over and over, at that period — the same stroke the entrance uses, handed a cycle and told to repeat. 0 is off. It needs an outline to draw: set a weight under Letters first, or there is no stroke for it to work on.

What it does once it has landed, for as long as it is up. Ambient cycle is how long one breath takes, Eye cycle how long one round of looking takes. Separate on purpose, and neither governs the other — a long, slowly-shifting rest comes from the two being different lengths, not from turning one of them down.

It arrives once, then rests. While it rests the eyes keep looking around whatever else is chosen — that is what makes it read as awake rather than parked. Loop length is the ambient cycle, Amount scales it, Loop ease decides whether it drifts evenly or accents each end, and Hold keeps it at its extremes for a beat instead of turning round the moment it arrives. Use Showing, under the viewer, to work on the arrival and the resting motion separately. Export, then add it in the admin panel as a Sleep mark.

Layout

Turn moves the whole arrangement; Turn X and Turn O rotate the letterforms themselves, separately, because an X reads as tilted at angles where an O still reads as upright. The eyes stay level whatever these do.

Settle turns the arrangement into where the letters come FROM: at 1 they travel out of the grid and land as the wordmark. Spread scales how far apart they start, Shuffle swaps which letter takes which cell, and Score strikes the line through — the top row of a grid, or the run of a stack or diagonal.

Where the letters end up. They travel into the arrangement as they arrive, so Start spread (under Entrance) is where they come from and this is where they land — the two combine rather than fight.

The frame follows the arrangement, so a grid crops to a square and a row to a band. Gap at 1 is the wordmark as drawn; Cell turn alternates direction letter to letter so a grid reads as tiles rather than a tilted block.

Eyes

1 — The eye

What the eye IS, before it does anything. Dome is the fish-eye: a pupil sits on a curved surface, so it foreshortens along the way it travelled and is fattest facing you.

2 — The lids

None gives an eye that cannot close — a pupil in an open counter, looking about, which is a character in its own right and what most marks want. It is not the same as setting Blinks to zero: that leaves the lids there, resting out of sight, still able to take a resting cover from an expression. With none, Blinking below has nothing to work with and goes dim.

A lid covers the eye rather than shrinking it, so the pupil stays full size behind it and an eye three-quarters closed still reads as an eye. Lid curve is the shape of its edge — a tight arc reads cartoonish, a wide one deadpan.

3 — Looking around

Reach to the edge is how far the pupil goes, and the only control that decides it — there was a second one, Look around, multiplied into this, which is the same quantity said twice. It follows Pupil size and Oval, so a bigger pupil has less room to move in. At 1 the pupil arrives exactly on the inside of the ring, where a cartoon eye goes when it is looking hard at something; past 1 it breaks through the letterform.

They dart and hold rather than drifting — a pupil that slides smoothly reads as drowsy, one that snaps and then sits still reads as looking at something. Stretch and swell are what it does WHILE travelling, and are gone again once it lands.

4 — Blinking — needs lids

Six shapes to start from. Each sets the lid curve, how deep it shuts, the anticipation and bounce, what the lower lid does, and how the time splits between closing, holding and opening — then everything stays editable. Picking one keeps the blink the LENGTH you already made it and only changes the split inside, so it will not undo your work on speed.

Snap comes from that split, not from a snappier curve: a lid that shuts in 90ms and takes 400ms to open reads fast. Close, hold and reopen are the blink itself and Blink speed scales all three. Anticipation is the small lift before the drop, bounce is how far past its rest it opens — the two that make a blink read as flesh rather than a shutter. Settle is open time guaranteed before the run ends, so the mark never finishes mid-blink.

6 — Arms

A drawn arm reaches in while the mark rests and handles a letter. Rubber hose — no elbow, no shoulder, just a tapering curve off the edge of the frame into a four-fingered glove. Outline only, in the mark's own ink, so it reads as drawn ON the scene rather than cut out of it.

One visit per round of the eye, so the two are always in step — which is what lets the eyes watch the hand without either side knowing the other's clock. Visit length is the share of the round it takes; the rest of the round the arm is off-frame and the eyes are back to looking around on their own. Which letter at −1 picks one off the glance seed.

The arm is anchored BEHIND the furthest letter on the side it comes from, so it threads out from behind the letterforms rather than sliding in from off-stage — and it only becomes visible where it clears them. Two arms reach from both sides at once, one hand to each edge. The hand is in front, because a hand taking hold of something has to be seen doing it. The grip lands on the near EDGE of the letter — a hand closing over the middle of a letterform is holding a plate, not picking something up.

Strokes draws the line more than once, each pass offset and weighted a little differently, which is what makes an outline look drawn rather than plotted. Wander bends the arm off its perfect arc, tapering to nothing at the wrist. Boil redraws that wander a few times a visit, the way a hand-drawn line changes every time it is drawn again — 0 holds one drawing, which is steadier and cheaper.

With two hands, a grab IS a swap: each hand takes its own letter — one over the top, one underneath — and they trade places. Same-glyph trades (O with O, X with X) leave the mark looking untouched, which is what lets the round loop clean; trading an X with an O changes the mark, so the loop's restart will show. The eyes follow the hands, land crossed, shake it off. Poke prods. Wave never touches.

5 — Eyebrows

Brows ride the glance: they travel with the pupil, they TIP as it looks aside — up on the side it is looking toward, down on the other — and they rise when it looks up. The tip is most of what makes a glance read as deliberate rather than as an eyeball sliding about.

Height is measured from the top of the PUPIL, so a bigger eye pushes its brows up rather than growing into them. Follows the eye is deliberately partial: a brow that tracked the pupil one for one would read as being dragged along by it rather than belonging to the same face. Brows only ever appear over an O — an X has no eye to have one over.

Texture maps

Drop in tileable images. Both want to be seamlessly tileable and square — 512 to 2048px. The colour map is the surface colour itself, evenly lit with no shadows baked in. The height map is greyscale, white high and black low; use PNG for it so compression noise does not turn into surface noise.

Colour map

Height map

Stacks on top of the procedural bumps under 3D, so you can use both.

Both maps

Shared lays one continuous texture behind the whole mark, so the letters move across it. Per shape maps it onto each shape's own box, so it scales and deforms with the letter as it pops, squashes and dents — and the height map tiles in that shape's space too. Per shape runs the shader once per shape rather than once for the mark.

Tile size is in the mark's own units — it is 1000 wide — so 250 gives four tiles across. Maps are stored in the settings, so saved versions and exports carry them and get correspondingly large.

3D & material

Off at depth 0. Shading is a real light on the letterforms; rotation is projected by hand, because browsers flatten 3D transforms inside SVG.

Shading

SVG filters read an 8-bit height map and multiply it by depth, which is why deep relief bands. WebGL does the same lighting in float and only quantises at the very end, so depth and a smooth surface stop fighting. The animation is unchanged either way — the SVG still drives it and WebGL only takes over the shading, so texture maps, the knockout and the 2D surface texture stay on the SVG path for now.

Depth is also what makes the surface band. The height map is 8-bit, and depth multiplies its steps — at 10 the surface is clean with no dither at all, by 34 the contours are hard to hide. If rings appear, bring depth down before reaching for dither.

Roundness is how far the surface is blurred before it is lit. Too low and the middle of a thick shape flattens out — that is what makes it look like a sticker.

Surface

Surface normals come off an 8-bit alpha, so a wide ramp has only 256 levels and the light turns them into contour rings. This jitters each pixel across a band and blurs it back — the blur is the part that adds precision. Drop it to 0 to see what it is fixing; push it too far and the surface goes soft.

Surface smoothing

The light terraces any hard edge in the height map, so each source of relief gets softened before it is lit. Smoothing is the blunt one — it goes over the combined surface and takes out whatever the other two leave.

Dents and swells pushed into the surface before it is lit — thumbprints in the clay. Large scale reads as hand-worked, small as orange peel.

Rotation

How far each letter is turned when it starts, unwinding as it lands.

Extrude

Stacks slices behind the front face along the Y angle, so the letter has thickness. With only a twist it collapses flat once the letter lands; with a hold rotation below it stays turned and keeps its depth. Needs one or the other to have an axis to extrude along — at zero degrees there is no depth to show.

Hold rotation

A real 3D turn on the whole mark, held rather than animated — for looking at the surface from another angle while you light it.

Stop motion

A held frame rate and a frame that never quite lands twice.

0 plays smooth. Anything above steps the whole animation to that many frames a second — 8 to 12 is the stop-motion range.

Nudges the frame between held frames, the way a rostrum never lines up twice.
